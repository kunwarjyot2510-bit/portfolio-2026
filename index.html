Beyond the screen

I work on problems that don't have an obvious solution yet, systems that grew complicated one reasonable decision at a time, until nobody fully understood them anymore.

Across railway infrastructure, smart infrastructure, and industrial software, my work has mostly looked the same underneath: simplify a fragmented workflow, connect systems that were never built to talk to each other, and give someone the ability to make a faster, more confident decision. The industries differ. The underlying problem rarely does.

How I work

Understanding before screens

Before Siemens, I interned at a company where the job really was just UI, taking a defined problem and designing the interface for it. When I joined Siemens straight out of that, my first instinct was still to think about screens first, because that's what UX had meant to me up to that point. It took a few projects to realize how much has to happen before a screen makes sense: understanding the systems underneath it, the people who'd actually use it, and why the process got complicated in the first place. That's where I start now, and it's a lesson I had to unlearn my own habits to get to.

Complexity deserves clarity, not hiding

I've always worked reasonably well with a bit of chaos. My room was messy as a kid, and honestly it still can be. There's a kind of convenience in chaos, I usually know where everything is, even if no one else would. I think about complex systems the same way. Before I try to simplify anything, I want to understand why it got complicated in the first place. There's usually a real reason. The fix is rarely to hide that complexity, it's to expose the right part of it, to the right person, at the right time.

Design the system, not just the screen

This became real for me on RailXplore Fusion. The job wasn't to design any single screen well, it was to bring a dozen separate diagnostic systems into something an operator could read as one thing. That's the project where I stopped thinking about UX as a set of screens and started thinking about it as a set of relationships between systems, and between people.

Invisible design is successful design

I learned this one secondhand, from a professor who kept coming back to it: the best designs are the ones nobody notices. One example stuck with me, a door with a flat plate tells you to push, a handle tells you to pull, no sign needed, no thinking required. That's still the bar I hold my own work to, even if I can't always claim I've hit it.
