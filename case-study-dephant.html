dephant

Turning fragmented product-lifecycle processes into one connected enterprise platform

Overview

dephant is an internal product-lifecycle management platform that brings component, material, procurement, risk, compliance, and supply-chain workflows into one web application. It gives teams a shared source of truth while reducing the need to move between disconnected tools, spreadsheets, and email threads.

The platform began around Bill of Materials (BOM) and gradually expanded to support Risk Assessment, the Cyber Resilience Act, Last Time Buy, and the Procurement Engineering Workbook.

My Role

I joined an existing BOM application with significant usability issues. I began by conducting a heuristic evaluation and improving the existing experience. As more use cases were added, my role expanded from redesigning one module to shaping how multiple workflows, users, and stakeholders could work together as one product ecosystem.

Primary users: Component Engineers, Electrical Engineers, Procurement Engineers, Product Owners, Project Managers, Cost Value Engineers, and other role-specific stakeholders across the five use cases.

  • Improved the existing BOM experience through heuristic evaluation, interaction redesign, and continuous feedback after release.
  • Mapped overlapping users and workflows so that related use cases could share information without becoming five disconnected applications.
  • Translated spreadsheet-heavy processes into structured product workflows while preserving the flexibility users relied on.
  • Defined information architecture and reusable patterns that could scale as Risk Assessment, CRA, LTB, and PE Workbook were added.
  • Aligned PMs, domain experts, users, and developers throughout design, delivery, feedback, and enhancement cycles.

The Problem

Before dephant, teams relied on a mixture of internal systems, Excel files, and email. Users compared multiple spreadsheets, maintained their own versions, re-entered information, communicated pricing through email, and tracked approvals across separate conversations.

The result was duplicated data, unclear ownership, limited transparency, and frequent version conflicts. For workflows such as Last Time Buy, these gaps also affected supply-chain decisions around components and materials approaching discontinuation.

The challenge was not simply to digitise an Excel sheet. It was to create enough structure for consistency and traceability without removing the flexibility that made spreadsheets useful in the first place.

The Approach

I worked use case by use case rather than attempting to redesign the entire ecosystem at once. BOM was improved and released first, followed by Risk Assessment. Each release generated feedback and enhancement requests that informed the next iteration. CRA and PE Workbook are both ongoing use cases; LTB is currently being developed, with PE Workbook following it.

This incremental approach allowed the platform to evolve through real usage while the shared architecture, navigation, data relationships, and reusable patterns were refined alongside it.

Early Personas

I facilitated a persona workshop to move the team from assumptions to a shared understanding of the people behind the workflow. We first listed the possible user groups, prioritised the primary roles together, and then organised their responsibilities, environments, technology proficiency, jobs-to-be-done, and pain points.

The research highlighted recurring needs such as visible request status, clearer explanations, reduced dependency on individual colleagues, and better links between BOM review results and design information.

Information Architecture

The original structure grew around BOM as a separate destination, while profile, membership, documentation, and review functions were distributed across different branches. As Risk Assessment was introduced, I reorganised the architecture around a shared home and reusable platform-level areas.

The revised structure made BOM Review and Risk Assessment visible as parallel use cases while keeping profile and membership functions consistent across the wider ecosystem.

The Hard Call

The central trade-off was between spreadsheet flexibility and product consistency. Excel allowed users to add columns, duplicate information, type free-form notes, and create personal workarounds. A structured application could improve traceability and reduce errors, but it could not preserve every informal behaviour.

The design therefore had to decide which flexibility supported real work and which flexibility created ambiguity. The aim was not to reproduce Excel inside a browser, but to keep the actions users genuinely depended on while introducing clearer ownership, statuses, validations, and shared information.

Impact

dephant is deployed and actively used. It has reduced dependence on disconnected tools and spreadsheets, improved transparency between stakeholders, and created a more reliable source of truth with fewer duplicate records and version conflicts.

The platform has grown from one BOM use case to five connected areas without requiring a separate product for each one. Feedback and enhancement requests have continued after every release, and those improvements have been incorporated into subsequent iterations.

Reflection

Improving an existing product can grow into ecosystem design. I joined to address usability issues in one BOM application. The work eventually required thinking across users, data, workflows, PMs, and releases rather than treating each screen or use case independently.

Replacing spreadsheets means replacing both their problems and their freedom. Structure improves consistency, transparency, and traceability, but it also removes some user-created flexibility. Good enterprise design requires understanding which parts of that freedom are essential before standardising the process.

Release is part of the research process. Shipping use cases sequentially, listening to users, and incorporating enhancement requests made the product stronger than attempting to define the entire ecosystem upfront.
