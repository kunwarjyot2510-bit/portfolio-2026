Controlguide DPM

Designing for railway operators applying speed restrictions in the field, not at a desk
View on the App Store Siemens product page

Overview

Controlguide DPM is an iPad app that railway operators use to apply temporary speed restrictions across a rail network. Unlike dephant and RailXplore Fusion, this project wasn't about ecosystems or dashboards. It was about getting one task right, every time, for people who aren't sitting at a desk when they do it.

My Role

I joined Controlguide at an early stage, taking it over from another designer, so much of the initial direction was already in place by the time I stepped in. From there, I owned the interaction design through to a shipped, published app, working alongside a PM, a product owner, an architect, and two fellow designers who supported the domain understanding work. I also went through domain training myself to get properly up to speed on how speed restrictions actually function operationally. The project ran roughly 7 to 9 months.

Primary Users: Railway operators working in the field, across six to seven distinct roles (including Describer, Reviewer, State Administrator, Transferer, and Verifier)

The Problem

Operators impose temporary speed restrictions for all kinds of reasons: maintenance, construction, bad weather, a safety concern that just came up. These aren't decisions someone makes at leisure. They're time-sensitive, and if the interaction goes wrong, the consequences aren't just "bad UX." They're operational.

So this project needed to answer a fairly narrow but important question: how do you design an interface that someone can use quickly and correctly while they're standing outside, in the sun, wearing gloves, mid-task, with pressure to move fast?

The Approach

We didn't have direct access to end users for this project — no field visits, no interviews with the operators themselves, and I want to be upfront about that rather than smooth it over. What we did have was domain training and the team's existing knowledge of the environment operators actually work in, and we used that to ground every interaction decision in real constraints rather than assumptions.

The design had to answer one question in practice: how does someone use this quickly and correctly while standing outside in the sun, wearing gloves, mid-task, with pressure to move fast? A few decisions came directly out of that:

  • Targets sized for error, not precision. Buttons and tap zones large enough that a gloved, slightly-off tap still lands correctly, rather than expecting the same accuracy as a bare finger on a phone.
  • Selection over typing. Choosing from a list or tapping a point on the map wherever possible, instead of free text — typing accurately with gloves on is genuinely hard.
  • One decision per screen. An operator interrupted mid-task, which is likely outdoors, can look back at the screen and immediately see where they left off, without reconstructing context.
  • Contrast that survives glare. High-contrast states chosen specifically for outdoor sunlight, not just for dark-mode consistency.
  • Friction where it matters, not everywhere. Routine actions stay fast; only the genuinely risky ones, like confirming a restriction, get deliberate extra distance or a confirmation step.

That's a real gap compared to ideal process, and I'd have preferred direct access to the people using this daily. But the constraints we designed for were well-documented and consistent enough that I'm confident the decisions were grounded in something real, not guesswork.

The Hard Call

Getting the physical constraints right meant trading away some things a desk-bound interface could afford:

  • Fewer precise gestures required, in exchange for larger, simpler touch targets that cost screen density.
  • High-contrast, limited color states, in exchange for some of the subtler status distinctions a quieter indoor interface might have used.
  • Short, linear flows, in exchange for flexibility — an operator moving fast in the field shouldn't need to navigate branching menus mid-task.

None of these were free choices. Each one traded some interface richness for something that mattered more given who'd actually be using it and where.

Impact

Controlguide shipped to production and is used by railway operators in the field, bringing six to seven previously separate roles into one application that replaced a process which used to run on paper forms passed between people by hand. The process is more transparent now — status is visible rather than inferred — and faster, since nothing has to physically travel between the people involved. I don't yet have volume or time-saved figures from the team, and I'd rather wait for real numbers than estimate ones I can't back up.

Reflection

Inheriting someone else's direction is its own skill — respecting decisions already made while still owning everything that shipped afterward.

Domain training is a real substitute for user access, not a full one. I'd rather say this project skipped field interviews than imply it didn't.

Designing for someone outdoors, under time pressure, with attention you can't assume, is a different problem than designing for someone at a desk. That's stayed with me on projects that have nothing to do with railways.

Requirements didn't stay still, and neither did the people setting them. The real skill wasn't defending a design — it was showing, concretely, why one approach served the operator better, until that became the obvious choice rather than my opinion versus theirs.
